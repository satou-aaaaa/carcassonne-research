Carcassonne by the Numbers

Compared with chess or Go, Carcassonne has barely been studied. For this page, the strongest AI on this site played 2,000 games against itself, and we measured the first-player advantage, where points come from, how often games turn around, what each tile is worth, and more. We also compare Carcassonne with chess, Go, shogi and other games.

1How big is the first-player advantage?

Over 2,000 games: first player 1,123 wins, second player 838 wins, 39 draws. The first player scored 57.1% (95% interval 54.9%–59.3%) and won by +4.0 points on average (±0.5). The first player has a clear edge (very unlikely to be chance).

Wins for the first and second player (2,000 games)

The main reason is the tile count. The 71 tiles after the start tile are placed in turn, so the first player places 36 and the second player 35. The first player also places the very last tile. Removing one tile so both place the same number almost erased the gap (section 9). Draws are rare: 1.9%.

Takeaway
  • As the second player, being a few points behind means the game is about even. There is no need to force risky moves.
  • The first player gets the last tile. In the endgame, count whether your opponent still has a turn left, and plan the order in which you complete things.
  • When playing with friends or family, play two games and swap who goes first to keep it fair.

2Scores and margins

The average score per player was 98. Winners averaged 106 and losers 90. The median margin was 14 points; 38% of games were decided by 10 points or less, and 22% by 5 or less.

Winning margins (5-point bins)

Takeaway

Even between strong AIs, more than one game in three ends within 10 points. A few points from a short road, or 3 points from a field at the end, decide the game.

3Where do the points come from?

Cities are the biggest source at 43% of all points; fields (farmers) gave 17%. What people often miss: points scored at the end of the game (unfinished cities, roads and monasteries, plus fields) make up 43% of the total.

Takeaway
  • Counting only completed points gives a badly wrong picture. Judge the score by the projected points, including unfinished features and fields.
  • Nearly half of monasteries end unfinished, but they still score one point per surrounding tile. The earlier you place one, the more its neighbours fill in.

4How the score develops, and comebacks

Average banked score (completed features) by turn. Scoring speeds up later as big cities get completed, and jumps at the very end when the final scoring is added.

Average score by turn (first player, second player)

The player leading on projected points (the score if the game ended now) went on to win 60% of the time in the opening (turn 17), 67% at halftime (turn 35), and 79% late in the game (turn 52). The lead changes hands 5 times per game on average. A player 15 or more points behind at halftime still came back to win in 18% of those games.

Projected lead at halftime vs. how often the leader wins

Takeaway

An early lead means little. Even if you are behind in the middle game, you can catch up with features that are about to close and with fields. Compare projected points, not banked points.

5Using meeples

The strong AI places a meeple about 16 times per player per game, on 45% of its turns. It can place more than twice its 7 meeples because it keeps completing features and getting them back. Even so, it had none in hand on 13% of its turns.

Average meeples in hand (first player, second player)

Where meeples were placed

Placements: cities 43%, roads 32%, monasteries 14%, fields 11%. At the end, each player still had 4.1 meeples on unfinished features on average, and 1.6 farmers that scored.

Takeaway

Keep meeples cycling: place, complete, place again. Use small cities and short roads that close quickly, and try not to run out before the endgame.

7Luck and skill

The luck in Carcassonne is which tile you draw and when. We played every tile order twice and compared. With the same tiles, the same player won only 55% of the time (100% if the tiles decided everything, 50% if they did not matter at all). About 17% of the spread in final margins can be put down to the tile order.

We also played AIs of different strength against each other, two games per tile order with seats swapped. The last column converts each score into the rating gap (Elo) it would mean in chess.

8Compared with other games

A Carcassonne turn offers about 81 moves on average (tile spot and rotation × meeple choice), and up to 444. Counting only the tile spot and rotation, it is 23. The game tree (all possible move sequences in a game) is about 10127, similar to chess. Adding the branching from the tile draw (about 17 kinds of tile) gives about 10209.

Takeaway
  • The first-player edge is about the same as in chess (White scores about 55%) or slightly larger. Unlike Go, there is no komi to balance it; tournaments only award ties to the second player (section 9).
  • Unlike chess or Go, there is luck in the tile draw. Still, each turn offers more choices than chess, and better players win more.
  • AI has surpassed the best humans at chess, Go and shogi, but there is no public record of that for Carcassonne yet. There is a lot left to study.

9How to remove the first-player advantage

In Go, White receives “komi”, extra points that offset Black’s advantage of moving first. To see whether the same works in Carcassonne, we changed the rules and had the AI play at least 1,000 games under each.

Tournament rule: “ties go to the second player”

Under the standard rules, tied players share the victory. But at the Japanese Championship and the German championship, a tie is a win for the second player. The “If ties go to the second player” column in the table counts games this way. Under the current rules the first player then wins 56.1% (or scores 57.1% when a draw counts as half a win). Because draws are rare to begin with, this lowers the first player's rate by only 1.0 points, so almost all of the first-player edge remains. Our estimate: if ties go to the second player and the second player also starts with 3 points, the first player wins 51.0%; with 4 points, 48.8%, roughly even.

The chart below estimates what happens if the second player’s bonus is added to the margins of games played under the current rules. 3 points is not enough, and 5 points tips the balance to the second player.

Takeaway
  • When you play a single game with friends or family, put one tile back in the box unseen before you start (35 tiles each). That almost erases the first-player edge.
  • Starting the second player’s scoring marker on 4 also shrinks the gap to within the margin of error.
  • Even the tournament rule “ties go to the second player” barely changes the first-player edge, because draws almost never happen.
  • 4 points is the figure for strong players. Between beginners the first-player edge may be smaller, so starting with 2 or 3 points is fine too.

How we measured

The AI uses the same settings as “Standard” on the play page (2000 searches per move with the latest evaluation function, gen975). Each of 1,000 tile orders was played twice with different move randomness (as of 2026-11-05). Score intervals are Wilson 95% confidence intervals; the first-player test is a binomial test excluding draws (p < 0.001). “Projected points” count unfinished features and fields as if the game ended at that moment. Matches between different AIs pair two games per tile order with seats swapped. In the rule-change games the AI knows the rule (komi is the second player's starting score; with 70 tiles the last tile stays face-down and unused). Numbers for human games may differ. Scripts: scripts/collect_game_stats.py and scripts/summarize_game_stats.py; results: docs/results/game_stats.json.
