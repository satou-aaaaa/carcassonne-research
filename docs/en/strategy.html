How to think to win

You know the rules but aren't sure what to think about when you place a tile? This guide is for you. It goes step by step from the core ideas through the tile mix and plans for each stage of the game to common mistakes.

1Core ideas

71 tilesplaced in one game (all 72 minus the start tile)
35–36 turnsper player in a two-player game
7 meeplesper player. How often you can reuse them decides the game
  • Think in point differences. In a two-player game, the bigger "your points − opponent's points" is, the closer you are to winning. A move that gains you 3 points is worth the same as one that denies your opponent 3. Handing your opponent 5 points is the same as losing 5 yourself.
  • Completing doubles the value. A completed city scores 2 per tile, an unfinished one only 1. A completed monastery scores 9; an unfinished one depends on how many neighbours are filled. Only roads score 1 per tile either way.
  • The sooner a meeple returns, the better. When a feature is completed the meeple comes back and can score again somewhere else. Keeping your 7 meeples from getting stuck is the fastest way to raise your score.

2Five things to learn first

For your first few games, these five are enough.

  1. Build small cities and close them quickly. Cities of 2–3 tiles are easy to close and reliably return points and meeples.
  2. Only put meeples on roads that will end soon. Roads are cheap, and if the ends aren't fixed the meeple never comes back.
  3. Put monasteries where the neighbours fill easily. Near the middle of the map, where there are already many tiles, they reach 9 points more easily.
  4. Keep 1–2 meeples in hand. You can use them later for big plays, such as joining an opponent's city or placing farmers late in the game.
  5. Before placing, check whether it helps your opponent. Completing their city gives them points and their meeple back. Compare your gain with theirs before you place.

3Know the tile mix

The contents of the tile bag are fixed. Once you know how many of each tile there are, you can work out how many of a given shape are left. Strong players are strong because they can count like this.

22 tileswith only roads and fields
6 tileswith a monastery

Counts are for the whole base game, including the start tile (city on top, road left and right).

4How easily holes fill

To complete a city you must fill every opening (open edge) with a tile. But the more tiles surround a hole, the fewer tiles fit it. Knowing which holes are hard to fill helps you both keep your own cities out of danger and make your opponent's cities hard to close.

  • Subtract the ones already played. The table counts the whole tile set. If tiles of that shape are already on the board, fewer remain.
  • In a two-player game, your opponent may draw it. Even if a fitting tile remains, your opponent will use it for themselves if they draw it. Treat holes with few fitting tiles as "almost never closing", and don't commit too many meeples to them.
  • Even simple holes have surprisingly few fits. A city opening surrounded only by fields can be filled by just 5 tiles (those with city on one edge only).

5Plans for the opening, middle and end game

Opening (first third) Build a base
  • Start your own small cities and close them.
  • When you draw a monastery, put it where it is easy to surround.
  • At most 1–2 farmers.
  • Don't use up all your meeples at once.
Middle game Engage your opponent
  • Join your opponent's big city and share it.
  • Fill in around your own monastery yourself.
  • Look for placements that leave hard-to-fill holes next to your opponent's cities.
  • Don't add openings to your own cities; close them early.
End game (last dozen or so tiles) Cash in
  • Don't put new meeples on cities or roads that won't close in time.
  • Put spare meeples in fields that touch many completed cities.
  • An unfinished monastery still scores for each neighbour.
  • Keep looking for moves that stop your opponent completing things, right up to the last tile.

6Joining and defending

Attack: join your opponent's city

You can't place directly in a city your opponent occupies, but you can start your own small city right next to it with a meeple, then connect the two with one tile. With equal meeples you both score in full; with more, only you score. The bigger their city, the more it is worth joining.

Attack: make your opponent's city hard to close

When placing next to an opening of your opponent's city, choose the rotation that leaves a hole with few fitting tiles in the table above. If you can create a hole no tile can fill, such as city · road · city · road, that city can never be completed. It drops from 2 points per tile to 1, and your opponent's meeple stays stuck until the end.

Defence: keep your cities from being joined or blocked

  • Keep few openings on your cities and close them when you can. The bigger a city grows, the bigger a target it becomes for joining and blocking.
  • If your opponent starts another city next to yours, they are planning to join. Close yours first, or shape it so the two can't connect.
  • Don't create holes with city on three sides yourself. Few tiles will fit.

7Farmer (field) basics

  • Fields are divided by roads and cities. The two sides of a road are separate fields. Before placing a farmer, check how far the field extends.
  • Choose fields that touch many cities likely to be completed. Only cities completed by the end count, 3 points each. A field touching several small cities is a strong spot.
  • Fields are decided by majority too. As with cities, in a connected field the player with more farmers scores, and with a tie both score in full. You can also join an opponent's big field from another field.
  • Farmers never come back. Place too many early and you'll run short of meeples for cities. One or two in the opening, the rest late.

9Glossary

Tile
A piece of the map; 72 in all. Draw one at a time and place it so the edges match.
Meeple
Your person-shaped piece; 7 per player. Its name changes with where it is placed.
Knight · thief · monk · farmer
Names for a meeple in a city, on a road, in a monastery and in a field.
Feature
A city, road, monastery or field. Connected parts count as one feature.
Shield
The coat of arms on some city tiles. Each adds 2 points to a completed city.
Opening
An open edge of a city or road with no tile next to it yet. When all are filled, the feature is complete.
Joining (merging)
Connecting separately built cities or fields so your meeple shares a feature with your opponent's.
Majority
When one feature has several meeples, the player with the most scores (all tied players score in full).
Uncompletable
A feature with a hole no tile can fill, so it can never be completed.
End-of-game scoring
Points for unfinished cities, roads, monasteries and farmers, counted after the tiles run out.

10How to practise

  1. Solve the beginner problems in the next-move quiz. You can try ideas from this page, like closing quickly and keeping meeples, in real positions.
  2. Play the AI (beginner mode). It shows how many points each of your moves lost. Reviewing only the costly ones is enough.
  3. Read the lessons from the AI. Diagrams from real games show joining moves and moves that make an opponent's city hard to close.
  4. Move on to the advanced quiz problems. They train you to think in point differences in tricky positions.

About this page

This page uses the current rules of the base game (72 tiles) and assumes two players. Tile counts and "tiles that fit a hole" are counted from this project's tile definitions (data/tiles.json). A tile "fits" if some rotation matches the edge types (city, road, field); whether the city connects inside the hole is not distinguished (scripts/build_strategy_page.py).
